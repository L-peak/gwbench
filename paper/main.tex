\title{When Does Deep Learning Beat Persistence for Groundwater-Level Forecasting? A Reproducible Benchmark and a Persistence-Anchored Residual Model}

\author{Zhaoyang Li\corref{cor1}}
\ead{3286533389@qq.com}
\affiliation{organization={Henan Polytechnic University}, city={Jiaozuo}, state={Henan}, country={China}}

\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{abstract}
Groundwater-level forecasting is increasingly tackled with deep learning, but
reported gains are difficult to compare because studies use different wells,
splits, horizons, and baselines. We present \texttt{gwbench}, a reproducible
benchmark built from public USGS daily depth-to-water records for 279 wells in
California, Nebraska, and Texas (2000--2024), with a 74-well clean core and
purged temporal splits. We compare persistence, seasonal naive, per-site
ridge, and a residual GRU. Persistence is strong at short
horizons (mean NSE 0.97 at one day). At 30 days the calibrated GRU is
statistically tied with persistence on mean NSE, and a fixed global blending
weight is worse than persistence in mean NSE, showing that site-specific
calibration---not blending alone---is essential. At 90 days the calibrated
model improves median NSE relative to persistence from $-0.18$ to $0.21$
(paired median $\Delta$NSE $+0.11$, 95\% CI 0.05--0.22) and mean KGE from 0.46
to 0.52 (95\% CI 0.01--0.11); mean NSE is $-0.24$ versus $-0.22$ and its
interval includes zero because one distribution-shift well dominates. A
per-site ridge baseline has higher median NSE and KGE than the GRU but much
heavier mean-error tails; the calibrated GRU reduces the most severe failures
of the raw GRU and ridge, but persistence remains the most conservative tail
baseline. We do not claim broader robustness. Missing-input stress tests are
asymmetric: 50\% random point masking pushes 30-day mean NSE below
persistence, whereas 90-day performance does not degrade under the same
protocol. Code and configurations are released for reproducible
comparison.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Groundwater is a primary water source for agriculture, ecosystems, and
municipal supply, and water-level observations are among the most direct
indicators of aquifer stress. Machine-learning (ML) models are now widely used
to forecast groundwater levels, often with recurrent architectures such as
LSTM \citep{hochreiter1997} and GRU \citep{cho2014}, or regional large-sample
models \citep{kratzert2019}; recent reviews document the breadth of the field
\citep{shen2018,khan2023,dey2025}. Reported
gains, however, are hard to compare: studies differ in well selection,
train/test splitting, forecast horizon, and---critically---the strength of the
baseline. Random train/test splits can leak information across nearby dates,
and persistence (the last observed value) is rarely reported even though it is
extremely competitive for slow-moving hydrological variables.

Prior groundwater-level ML studies have used feed-forward networks, support
vector regression, random forests, LSTMs, GRUs, and hybrid decomposition
methods, usually for one well or a small cluster
\citep{khan2023,dey2025}. Direct comparisons of LSTM, CNN, and NARX models on
monitoring wells have improved practice, but remain regional and rarely report
a persistence baseline on identical anchors \citep{wunsch2021}. Global
entity-aware groundwater models have also been explored, but their evaluation
remains sensitive to entity definitions and split design \citep{heudorfer2024}.
Reviews emphasize that reported accuracy is strongly study-specific and that
preprocessing, split design, and baseline choice are rarely standardized
\citep{rajaee2019,khan2023,boo2024,shen2018}. Temporal autocorrelation also makes
random or weakly separated splits optimistic, a problem well known in
ecological and hydrological cross-validation
\citep{roberts2017,bergmeir2012}. In surface-water hydrology, large-sample
benchmarks and regional LSTMs have shown that a common dataset and split can
change conclusions about model skill
\citep{addor2017,kratzert2019,reichstein2019,nearing2021}. We are not aware of a comparable
open, multi-well daily groundwater benchmark with fixed splits, explicit
missingness, and a persistence baseline. This paper provides such a benchmark
for three U.S. states and one widely measured parameter, and uses it to
characterize when a deep model actually improves on persistence.

This paper makes four contributions. First, we release a reproducible
benchmark for daily groundwater-level forecasting built entirely from public
USGS data, with three documented panels (279, 89, and 74 wells), temporal
splits, and a quality-control (QC) pipeline. Second, we evaluate persistence,
seasonal naive, and per-site ridge regression on exactly the same anchors as
the neural model, and quantify how screening changes baseline skill across
panels. Third, we isolate the source of the neural model's gains with a fusion
ablation: raw residual GRU, a fixed global blending weight, and the proposed
per-site validation-fitted weight. The ablation shows that a global weight
cannot rescue low-variance wells; site-specific calibration is the essential
ingredient. Fourth, we report a horizon-dependent conclusion that is usually
missing from the literature: at 30 days the calibrated model is statistically
tied with persistence on mean NSE, whereas at 90 days it improves median NSE
and mean/median KGE, but not mean NSE, whose confidence interval includes zero.
The benchmark also shows that a simple per-site ridge regression attains
higher median NSE and KGE than the GRU at both horizons but has much heavier
mean-error tails; the calibrated GRU reduces the most severe failures relative
to the raw GRU and ridge, but persistence remains the most conservative tail
baseline. We support these claims with paired site-level bootstrap intervals,
per-site distributions, repeated missing-data stress tests, and a cross-panel
sensitivity analysis.

We position this work as a benchmark and reproducibility study rather than a
new network architecture. Recent reviews document the rapid growth of
groundwater-level ML \citep{khan2023}; our contribution is the combination of
an open, standardized pipeline, honest evaluation against persistence, and
practical guidance for when a simple residual model does---and does not---beat
a strong baseline. Residual forecasting and persistence blending are known
techniques; the novelty is the benchmark protocol, the controlled fusion
ablation, and the empirical characterization of failure modes, not a new
algorithm.

\section{Data}

\subsection{USGS records and selection}

All observations come from the USGS National Water Information System (NWIS)
\citep{usgs2026}. We use parameter code 72019 (depth to water level, feet below
land surface) and statistic code 00001 (daily maximum). Wells are selected from
California, Nebraska, and Texas---the states with the largest number of long
daily records in our initial catalog scan---subject to three criteria: at least
3,650 observations, a first-to-last span of at least 10 years, and a record
extending to at least 2015. The final collection contains 279 wells and
1,581,469 observed daily values from 2000--2024.

\subsection{Panels and quality control}

We build a complete daily panel for each site and mark observed versus missing
days explicitly. Three panels are used (Table~\ref{tab:panels}): the full set,
a core set with at least 70\% observed coverage, and a clean core that also
removes wells with more than five day-to-day jumps larger than 5 ft. Sentinel
values (100000.x) are removed before panel construction. We flag rather than
silently correct jumps and long gaps, because they may reflect real datum or
sensor changes. All main experiments use the clean core; the full and screened
core panels are provided for extension and stress testing.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Splits and anchors}

We use a purged temporal split: training data through 2015, validation data
2016--2018, and test data 2019--2024. An anchor is the last day $t$ of a
365-day input window; the forecast target is day $t+h$. An anchor is valid only
if (i) the target day is observed and (ii) the input window contains at least
30 observed values. Missing inputs are allowed and are represented by the mask
channel; anchors with a missing target are excluded rather than imputed.
Training anchors use a stride of three days, while validation and test anchors
use every day. To prevent target leakage across split boundaries, a training
anchor is retained only if its target date satisfies $t+h \leq 2015\text{-}12\text{-}31$,
and a validation anchor is retained only if $t+h \leq 2018\text{-}12\text{-}31$.
Test anchors satisfy $t \geq 2019\text{-}01\text{-}01$, so their targets are
always in the test period. No target from a later period is used for early
stopping or calibration. After purging, the 30-day model uses 116{,}415
training anchors (stride 3) and 76{,}493 validation anchors; the 90-day model
uses 114{,}941 and 72{,}462. The clean core yields 103{,}956 test anchors at
30 days and 99{,}750 at 90 days before per-site metric filtering.

\subsection{Baselines}

\textbf{Persistence} predicts the last observed value before the forecast
anchor. \textbf{Seasonal naive} predicts the value 365 days before the target
date. A \textbf{per-site ridge regression} uses the last value, rolling means
(7, 30, 90, 365 days), rolling standard deviations, and day-of-year sine/cosine
features; a linear time trend is excluded to reduce extrapolation. Ridge is
fitted separately for each well on training anchors and evaluated on exactly
the same anchors as the GRU. All deterministic baselines use the same target
filtering as the neural model.

\subsection{Global residual GRU}

The main model is a two-layer GRU \citep{cho2014} shared across wells. Each
input window spans 365 days and contains four channels: the normalized value,
an observation mask, and day-of-year sine and cosine features. A learned
8-dimensional site embedding is concatenated to every time step. The final GRU
hidden state (last time step) is passed through layer normalization and a
linear projection to produce one scalar residual prediction; no output
activation is used (Table~\ref{tab:architecture}). The target is the residual from
the last observed value in the window:
\begin{equation}
  y_{\text{res}} = \frac{y_{t+h} - \mu_s}{\sigma_s}
  - \frac{y_{\text{last}} - \mu_s}{\sigma_s},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_s$ and $\sigma_s$ are the training-period mean and standard deviation
of site $s$, and $y_{\text{last}}$ is the last observed value in the input
window. Site-wise z-scoring is used instead of a global scale because wells
differ by orders of magnitude in depth and variance; a global scale would let
deep, high-variance wells dominate the MSE. The residual target is
dimensionless, so the same model can be trained across sites. This forces a
zero output to reproduce persistence and lets the model learn only the
correction. We use hidden size 64, dropout 0.2, Adam with
learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 256, MSE loss, gradient clipping, and early
stopping on validation RMSE. Training anchors use a stride of three days; test
anchors use every day.

\subsection{Persistence blending}

For each site, we fit a scalar $\alpha_s \in [0,1]$ on validation predictions:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\text{cal}} = \hat{y}_{\text{pers}} +
  \alpha_s \left(\hat{y}_{\text{GRU}} - \hat{y}_{\text{pers}}\right).
\end{equation}
The closed-form least-squares solution is clipped to $[0,1]$; a site whose GRU
is worse than persistence receives $\alpha_s=0$ and falls back to persistence.
This step uses validation data only and is applied unchanged to all test
periods and missingness levels. If a site has fewer than 30 validation pairs or
zero predictor variance, $\alpha_s$ is set to zero. Because the validation
period is also used for early stopping, Appendix~A refits
$\alpha_s$ on 2016--2017 and 2018 only; median NSE and KGE are stable across
windows, while mean NSE is calibration-sensitive.

\subsection{Fusion ablation}

To separate the contribution of the GRU correction from the contribution of
site-specific weighting, we compare four strategies on identical test anchors:
(i) persistence ($\alpha=0$), (ii) the raw residual GRU ($\alpha=1$), (iii) a
fixed global weight equal to the median validation-fitted $\alpha_s$ for that
seed, and (iv) the proposed per-site $\alpha_s$. The fixed weight is refitted
per seed and lies between 0.62 and 0.69 at 30 days and between 0.64 and 0.73 at
90 days. This ablation answers a direct question: does a single global blend
recover the benefit of calibration, or is the site-specific weight necessary?

\subsection{Metrics and robustness tests}

We report RMSE, MAE, Nash--Sutcliffe efficiency (NSE)
\citep{nash1970}, and Kling--Gupta efficiency (KGE) \citep{gupta2009}, as both
mean and median across wells. NSE is sensitive to absolute errors on
low-variance wells, whereas KGE balances correlation, bias, and variability;
reporting both prevents a single metric from driving the conclusion. Per-site
metrics require at least 30 paired test anchors. Aggregate means and medians are computed over wells; neural results
are reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds 1--3. For paired
comparisons we first average each site's metric over seeds, then bootstrap
wells 10{,}000 times with a fixed seed (20261029) and report percentile 95\%
confidence intervals for the mean and median of the per-site difference. These
intervals quantify between-well uncertainty conditional on the three evaluated
training seeds; they do not fully characterize variability under different
random initializations. Seed-level means and standard deviations are reported
separately. The median of the paired differences is not the difference of the
two marginal medians, and we report the former when making inference.

For the missing-data stress test, we randomly hide 10\%, 30\%, or 50\% of the
observed input values in the 365-day window at test time without retraining;
targets are unchanged. Because a single mask draw can be noisy, each fraction
is repeated with five mask seeds (1--5) on a stride-7 test subset (every
seventh test anchor), and we report the mean and standard deviation across
draws. The stride-7 subset is used only for computational tractability; its
metrics are not directly comparable to the full test set. For the cross-panel
analysis, persistence, seasonal naive, and ridge are re-evaluated on the full,
screened-core, and clean-core panels; the GRU is trained only on the clean core
and is therefore not directly comparable across panels.

\subsection{30 days: a statistical tie with persistence}

Table~\ref{tab:models} summarizes the 30- and 90-day results. At 30 days the
raw residual GRU has a median NSE of 0.605 but a mean NSE of $-1.527$ because
of a few catastrophic wells. Calibration removes these failures and yields a
mean NSE of $0.587 \pm 0.007$ versus 0.573 for persistence; the paired
site-level bootstrap 95\% confidence interval for mean $\Delta$NSE is
$[-0.056, 0.059]$, so the mean difference is not statistically distinguishable
from zero. Median NSE improves from 0.729 to $0.768 \pm 0.008$, but mean KGE is
slightly lower ($0.766 \pm 0.005$ vs 0.784) and its interval includes zero. We
therefore describe the 30-day result as a tie on mean NSE, not as an ML win.
Seed-averaged per-site changes are mostly small: 47/74 wells have positive
$\Delta$NSE, 25 negative, and 2 zero; the median $\Delta$NSE is $+0.006$
(95\% CI $0.000$--$0.022$), while the mean is $+0.014$ (95\% CI $-0.056$ to
$0.059$). The mean $\Delta$KGE is $-0.018$ (95\% CI $-0.043$ to $0.000$), and
the median is $-0.002$ (95\% CI $-0.012$ to $0.002$). This confirms that the
30-day result is a tie, not a win.

\subsection{Ridge baseline}

A per-site ridge regression with lag and rolling features (last value; 7-, 30-,
90-, and 365-day rolling means and standard deviations; day-of-year terms; no
linear time trend) was evaluated on exactly the same test anchors as the GRU
(Table~\ref{tab:models}).
It achieved median NSE 0.773 at 30 days and 0.339 at 90 days, compared with
persistence 0.729 and $-0.181$. Its mean NSE was 0.280 at 30 days and $-1.713$
at 90 days, showing that even a linear model can be dominated by a few trending
or low-variance wells. Median KGE was 0.853 and 0.632, compared with 0.822 and
0.462 for persistence. Seasonal naive is far worse at both horizons (mean NSE
$-2.995$ and $-3.437$). The ridge baseline therefore improves the median but is
not robust in the mean. Because ridge's median NSE (0.773 and 0.339) is higher
than the calibrated GRU's (0.768 and 0.210), the GRU's independent contribution
is not median accuracy: it is a reduction in the most severe failures relative
to the raw GRU and ridge. Persistence still has fewer deep failures than the
calibrated GRU, so the GRU is not uniformly more robust. A benchmark that
reported only medians would favor ridge, while one that reported only means
would favor the calibrated GRU. Reporting both, plus per-site distributions and
tail counts, is therefore essential; this is one of the main lessons of the
benchmark.

\subsection{90 days: robust median and KGE gains}

At 90 days the calibrated model improves the median and KGE point estimates
(Table~\ref{tab:models}), but not mean NSE. Median NSE rises from $-0.181$ to
$0.210 \pm 0.039$, mean KGE from 0.456 to $0.519 \pm 0.004$, and median KGE
from 0.463 to $0.573 \pm 0.015$; mean NSE is $-0.244 \pm 0.008$, slightly below
persistence ($-0.218$). Seed-averaged per-site changes are asymmetric: 51/73
wells have positive $\Delta$NSE, 18 negative, and 4 zero; the median
$\Delta$NSE is $+0.107$ (95\% CI $0.048$--$0.225$), while the mean is $-0.027$
(95\% CI $-0.531$ to $0.262$). The mean $\Delta$KGE is $+0.063$ (95\% CI
$0.010$ to $0.109$), and the median is $+0.023$ (95\% CI $0.010$ to $0.076$).
The median NSE and both KGE intervals are the statistically robust results; the
mean NSE interval includes zero and its point estimate is negative. One well
(\texttt{413156098591201}) undergoes a validation-to-test shift: across seeds
its calibrated NSE ranges from $-14.45$ to $-17.57$, while persistence is
$-0.71$. Excluding that single well, the seed-averaged calibrated mean NSE is
$-0.025$ versus $-0.211$ for persistence, and mean KGE is $0.539$ versus
$0.457$. We therefore report median NSE and KGE as the robust 90-day results
and treat the outlier explicitly.

\paragraph{Tail diagnostics}
To avoid equating a better mean with general robustness, we count severe
per-site failures. At 90 days, the number of wells with NSE below $-1$ is 9
for the calibrated GRU, 7 for persistence, 11 for ridge, and 25 for the raw
GRU; the corresponding minimum NSE values are $-16.0$, $-3.5$, $-37.9$, and
$-228.4$. At 30 days the counts are 2, 1, 6, and 9, respectively. The
calibrated GRU therefore removes many of the extreme failures of the raw GRU
and ridge, but persistence still has fewer deep failures and a much smaller
worst case. The evidence supports a limited claim---fewer severe failures than
the learned alternatives---not uniform robustness or superiority over
persistence.

\subsection{Why calibration is necessary}

The raw GRU is unstable on low-variance wells. On 30-day forecasts its mean NSE
is $-1.527$ despite a median of 0.605; on 90-day forecasts the corresponding
values are $-9.637$ and $-0.038$. The per-site blending weight fixes this:
across seeds, $\alpha_s>0$ for 60--69 of 74 wells at 30 days and 63--67 of 74
wells at 90 days, with the remainder falling back to persistence
(Figure~\ref{fig:alpha}). The calibration is therefore not cosmetic; it is what
makes a global residual model usable across heterogeneous wells.

A fixed global weight does not reproduce this benefit (Table~\ref{tab:fusion}).
At 30 days, the median validation-fitted weight yields mean NSE $-0.325$ and
median NSE 0.658, both worse than persistence (0.573 and 0.729); at 90 days it
yields mean NSE $-4.661$ and median NSE 0.070, far below the per-site result
($-0.244$ and 0.210). A single scalar cannot down-weight the low-variance wells
that dominate the mean without also shrinking the correction on wells where the
GRU is useful. The ablation therefore isolates site-specific calibration, not
the GRU alone or blending in general, as the ingredient that makes the residual
model work. Refitting $\alpha_s$ on 2016--2017 or 2018 only leaves median NSE
and mean KGE within about 0.05, but 90-day mean NSE ranges from $-0.449$ to
$-0.025$ (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:calwindows}); mean NSE is therefore sensitive
to the calibration window as well as to the outlier, while the median/KGE
conclusions are stable.

\subsection{Missing-data robustness}

On a stride-7 test subset, the response to missing inputs is asymmetric
(Table~\ref{tab:missing}, Figure~\ref{fig:missing}). At 30 days, mean NSE falls
from 0.637 at 0\% masking to 0.572 at 50\%, median NSE from 0.780 to 0.727, and
mean KGE from 0.774 to 0.775. At 50\% masking the 30-day model is therefore
slightly below persistence on the same stride-7 anchors (mean NSE 0.615,
median 0.744, KGE 0.786). At 90 days, by contrast, mean NSE rises from $-0.031$
to 0.035, median NSE from 0.207 to 0.265, and mean KGE from 0.535 to 0.559;
masking does not degrade performance at this horizon. The mask-to-mask
standard deviation is at most 0.004 in mean NSE and 0.002 in mean KGE. The
stride-7 protocol is used for computational tractability and is not directly
comparable to the full test-set metrics in Table~\ref{tab:models}. The
experiment removes isolated observations uniformly at random. The apparent
improvement at 90 days under random point masking should not be interpreted as
a beneficial effect of missing observations. One plausible mechanism is that
masking attenuates the learned residual correction toward persistence,
reducing errors at sites where the correction is less reliable; we did not test
this mechanism directly. As a second stress test, we removed one contiguous
block of 30, 60, or 90 days from each input window (three mask seeds, stride 7,
seed-1 model). At 30 days, mean NSE
falls from 0.637 to 0.626/0.619/0.613 for block lengths 30/60/90, median NSE
is stable at 0.779, and mean KGE falls from 0.774 to 0.772/0.771/0.770. At 90
days, mean NSE falls from $-0.031$ to $-0.076$/$-0.085$/$-0.091$, median NSE is
0.123/0.163/0.197, and mean KGE remains 0.536--0.537; all 90-day means remain
well above persistence ($-0.221$). Contiguous blocks are therefore less
damaging than 50\% random point masking at 30 days, but still degrade mean NSE
at 90 days. Real groundwater gaps can contain multiple blocks or instrument
outages; neither experiment covers all operational missingness mechanisms.

The training stride changes the number of training windows but not the test-day

\section{Discussion}

Three practical lessons emerge. First, persistence is an extremely strong
baseline for daily groundwater levels at short horizons. Studies that report
large 30-day gains should report persistence on the same temporal split; our
multi-seed analysis shows that a calibrated global GRU is statistically tied
with persistence at 30 days. Second, residual targets and site-specific blending
are necessary for a global model to remain usable across heterogeneous wells.
The uncalibrated GRU's catastrophic failures are not rare edge cases:
low-variance wells are common, and a single bad well can dominate a mean
metric. A fixed global weight does not solve this problem (Table~\ref{tab:fusion});
the calibration must be fitted per well. Third, the
useful signal appears at longer horizons. At 90 days, persistence is already
poor (mean NSE $-0.218$), and the calibrated model improves median NSE, mean
KGE, and median KGE across seeds, although mean NSE does not improve and its
confidence interval includes zero; one well drives much of the mean behavior.
The state-level table and the per-site win/loss counts reinforce a more nuanced
reading: the 90-day result is a distributional improvement (median and KGE)
rather than a uniform mean gain. At 90 days, 51/73 wells improve on NSE, 18
worsen, and 4 are unchanged; the mean is dominated by a few wells. We therefore
recommend that benchmark users report the full distribution, not only a mean.
The ridge comparison makes the point concrete: ridge has the better median at
both horizons but far worse mean errors, so neither model dominates. The
calibration-window sensitivity further shows that mean NSE ranges from $-0.449$
to $-0.025$ across windows while median NSE and mean KGE remain within about
0.05. Reported gains should therefore be interpreted as conditional on the
selected calibration protocol, not as universally stable improvements. The
calibrated GRU reduces the most severe failures relative to the raw GRU and
ridge, but persistence remains the most conservative tail baseline; a benchmark
that reports only one aggregate would conceal this trade-off.

The outlier well illustrates a broader evaluation issue. Calibration weights
are fitted on 2016--2018 and applied to 2019--2024; one well experiences a
validation-to-test shift that neither the GRU nor the blending weight
anticipates. This argues for reporting median metrics, win rates, and
per-site behavior alongside means, and for treating calibration as a
validation-dependent procedure whose robustness should be checked.

\subsection{Benchmark protocol and future models}

Table~\ref{tab:benchmarkcompare} summarizes the distinction from representative
groundwater ML studies: gwbench fixes the panel, target dates, anchors, and
baseline ladder, and releases the configurations needed to reproduce each
number. The comparison is qualitative and is not a systematic review.
We recommend that future work on gwbench (i) use the temporal split; (ii)
report persistence on the same anchors; (iii) report mean and median NSE/KGE,
win rates, and confidence intervals; (iv) fit any calibration on validation
only; (v) inspect per-site outliers; (vi) include missing-data stress tests;
and (vii) release code and data. The current GRU is a baseline, not the
endpoint; the benchmark is designed to be extended with new models and new
regions.

\subsection{Site embeddings and unseen wells}

The current model uses a learned site embedding and is therefore evaluated on
wells seen during training. For an unseen well the embedding is undefined, so
the present results should not be interpreted as a leave-well-out transfer
test. A proper cross-site experiment requires static predictors such as well
depth, aquifer, land cover, and climate, and is left for future work; the
benchmark repository includes the split machinery needed for that study.

\subsection{Climate covariates and physical limits}

The model is univariate apart from calendar features. It cannot represent
recharge, pumping, evapotranspiration, or storage changes; at 90 days these
omissions likely limit absolute skill. Adding precipitation, potential
evapotranspiration, and GRACE-derived storage anomalies is the most promising
route to improving the 90-day ceiling. The gains reported here come from
persistence anchoring and validation calibration, not from external drivers.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The benchmark covers three states and one parameter/statistic; the clean core
has 74 wells. The calibration weight is fitted on a validation period that is
also used for early stopping; the sensitivity analysis in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:calwindows}
shows that median NSE and KGE are stable to the calibration window, while mean
NSE is not. Bootstrap intervals are conditional on seeds 1--3 and do not
include initialization variance; seed-level standard deviations are reported
separately, and more seeds would tighten this uncertainty. Performance can also
degrade under distribution shift. USGS records contain
datum and sensor changes that we flag and filter but do not physically correct.
The univariate design and site-embedding limitation are discussed above and
define the main directions for future work.

\section{Conclusions}

We presented \texttt{gwbench}, a reproducible public-data benchmark for daily
groundwater-level forecasting, and a persistence-anchored residual GRU with
per-site calibration. At 30 days, the calibrated model is statistically tied
with persistence on mean NSE, and a fixed global blending weight is worse than
persistence in mean NSE; at 90 days, the per-site calibrated model improves
median NSE relative to persistence from $-0.181$ to 0.210 (paired median gain
0.107, 95\% CI 0.048--0.225) and mean KGE from 0.456 to 0.519 (95\% CI
0.010--0.109), although mean NSE is $-0.244$ versus $-0.218$ and its
confidence interval includes zero because one distribution-shift well dominates
the mean. A per-site ridge baseline has higher median NSE and KGE than the GRU
at both horizons but much worse mean errors; the calibrated GRU avoids the most
severe failures of the raw GRU and ridge, although persistence remains the most
conservative tail baseline and we do not claim broader robustness. The
uncalibrated GRU fails
catastrophically on low-variance wells, and the site-specific validation-fitted
weight is essential; a global weight does not reproduce the gain. Missing-input
behavior is horizon-dependent: at 30 days, 50\% random masking pushes mean NSE
below persistence, whereas at 90 days no systematic degradation is observed
under the tested point-masking protocol. We therefore do not claim robustness
to temporally clustered or operational missingness. We hope the benchmark
encourages stronger baselines, purged temporal splits, and per-site reporting
in groundwater ML.

\section{Reproducibility details}
\label{app:repro}

\paragraph{Data retrieval}
Daily values were retrieved in October 2026 from the USGS Water Data OGC API
(collections \texttt{field-measurements} and \texttt{daily}; parameter code
72019, statistic code 00001) with the legacy daily-values service as a batched
fallback when the OGC endpoint rate-limited requests. The requested period was
2000-01-01 to 2024-12-31. Wells were retained if they had at least 3{,}650
observations, a first-to-last span of at least 10 years, and a record extending
to at least 2015. Sentinel values of the form 100000.x were removed; day-to-day
jumps larger than 5 ft were flagged, and wells with more than five such jumps
were excluded from the clean core. The full, screened-core, and clean-core
panels contain 279, 89, and 74 wells (Table~\ref{tab:panels}). A per-well
manifest with state, coverage, jump count, gap statistics, and panel
membership is provided as \texttt{docs/site\_manifest.csv} in the repository.

\paragraph{Model and training}
The GRU has two layers, hidden size 64, dropout 0.2, and a learned site
embedding of the same dimension. Each input window is 365 days with four
channels (site-normalized value, observation mask, sine and cosine of
day-of-year). The model is trained with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$), batch
size 256, MSE loss, gradient clipping at 1.0, at most 40 epochs, and early
stopping with patience 5 on validation RMSE. Training anchors use stride 3 and
validation/test anchors stride 1; training and validation anchors are purged
at target-date boundaries (Section 3.1). The residual target is defined in
Equation~(1), so a zero output reproduces persistence. The 30- and 90-day
models are trained separately with seeds 1, 2, and 3. The same split and panel
are used for all seeds.

\paragraph{Calibration}
For each well, $\alpha_s$ is the clipped least-squares slope of the validation
target error on the GRU-minus-persistence difference, fitted only on validation
anchors with at least 30 paired predictions. The fitted weight is then applied
unchanged to all test anchors and missingness levels.

\paragraph{Calibration-window sensitivity}
The validation period is also used for early stopping. To test whether the
fitted weights are sensitive to this dual use, we refit $\alpha_s$ on
2016--2017, on 2018 only, and on the full 2016--2018 period (Table~\ref{tab:calwindows}).
The per-site weights are correlated across windows (full versus early
0.88--0.96; full versus 2018 0.69--0.82). Median NSE and mean KGE change by at
most 0.05 across windows, whereas 90-day mean NSE ranges from $-0.449$ to
$-0.025$. This reinforces the paper's choice of median NSE and KGE as robust
endpoints and shows that mean NSE is sensitive to the calibration window as
well as to the outlier.

\paragraph{Statistical protocol}
Per-site metrics are computed only for wells with at least 30 paired test
anchors. Aggregate means and medians are taken over wells; neural results are
reported as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over training seeds. Paired
confidence intervals resample wells with replacement 10{,}000 times using
NumPy's \texttt{default\_rng} with seed 20261029; the reported interval is the
2.5th--97.5th percentile of the bootstrap distribution. As a sensitivity
check, a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples both training seeds and wells
gives 30-day mean/median $\Delta$NSE $+0.014$ (95\% CI $-0.026$ to $0.047$)
and $+0.010$ ($0.004$ to $0.022$), and 90-day mean/median $\Delta$NSE $-0.027$
($-0.306$ to $0.192$) and $+0.100$ ($0.057$ to $0.166$). These intervals are
consistent with the seed-conditional results; with only three seeds the seed
dimension is coarse, so the site bootstrap remains the primary inference. The
missing-data
experiment repeats each masking fraction with five mask seeds on a stride-7
test subset and reports mean $\pm$ standard deviation across draws.
